\title{Learning Region-Specific Copulas from\\Marginal Constraints via Distribution-Level Diffusion}


\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\onehalfspacing

\begin{abstract}
Population synthesis from aggregate constraints requires recovering how demographic attributes co-occur within each region, not just how common each attribute is marginally. Existing methods can match target marginals, but they usually rely on shared seed distributions or other low-dimensional summaries that leave region-specific dependence weakly identified. We address this problem by training a conditional diffusion model directly on regional joint probability vectors, treating each observed region as one training example and conditioning generation on low-dimensional census summaries. We find that regional dependence is strongly heterogeneous across the United States and that the proposed model consistently improves reconstruction accuracy over a global-seed IPF benchmark, with the advantage remaining stable under held-out geographic validation. Controlled ablations further show that pairwise cross-tabulations contribute substantially more than one-dimensional marginals alone, identifying constraint informativeness rather than model capacity alone as the main driver of dependence recovery. These results show distribution-level diffusion as a practical route to learning region-specific copulas from aggregate constraints and suggest that future progress will depend more on richer conditioning statistics than on larger generative models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The creation of a synthetic population by reconstructing the joint distribution of demographic attributes within regions from aggregated data (i.e., data reporting regional marginal distributions of individual attributes) has long been regarded as a foundation of urban simulation, transportation modeling, and policy analysis \cite{mueller2011,barthelemy2013,la2025,chapuis2022}. However, aggregated data drastically under-specify the full picture from a statistical perspective. For example, when creating a synthetic population with five attributes and four values per attribute, the joint distribution contains more than one thousand potential combinations, whereas current methods typically rely on one-dimensional marginals alone. As a result, the underlying dependence structure---that is, the copula \cite{sklar1959} describing how multiple attributes co-occur---may be missed. The resulting synthetic population can therefore contain internal bias, such as assigning an impossible combination of attributes to a single synthetic individual.

Recovering a joint distribution from marginals alone is inherently difficult because the copula is hard to reconstruct \cite{frogner2019}. Existing population-synthesis methods nevertheless attempt to address this problem through two broad strategies: synthetic reconstruction and combinatorial optimization \cite{chapuis2022}. In the synthetic-reconstruction family, methods such as Hierarchical Sampling (HS) \cite{jiang_large-scale_2024}, Iterative Proportional Fitting (IPF) \cite{predhumeau_synthetic_2023}, and Iterative Proportional Updating (IPU) \cite{ipu2009} recover joint distributions from aggregated data (e.g., census-tract-level data) by matching target marginals while preserving the seed copula structure \cite{choupani2016,lovelace2015}. In the combinatorial-optimization family \cite{harland2012}, one extends real individual-level microdata or adjusts existing synthetic individuals to match target joint distributions through fitness evaluation and optimized sampling procedures.

However, these methods share a common limitation: the copula structure is extracted from aggregated data or real individual-level microdata and then applied uniformly across regions. This works when regions are demographically similar, but when regions are heterogeneous, a fixed copula can misrepresent local dependence and introduce internal bias into the resulting synthetic population \cite{roxburgh2025}. A related issue is spatial non-stationarity \cite{krupskii2018,mondal2024,bastin2023}, which fails to reconstruct the distribution of spatial heterogeneity related to the different combinations of demographic attributes. For example, a university region with a distinctive ``young + low-income + high-education'' composition pattern does not share the same age--income--education dependence pattern as a manufacturing region. To demonstrate this spatial heterogeneity, we extract regional dependence structures across the 2,456 Public Use Microdata Areas (PUMAs) in the United States and compare each region with the national average. The mean total variation distance (TVD) is 0.19, which is far from negligible and far from uniform across regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:heterogeneity}).

To handle such copula structures, optimization methods such as integer least squares programming have been used to create synthetic populations for the United States from Census Block Group data \cite{lin2023}. Parametric approaches instead use statistical inference to learn the joint relationship among hidden attributes in the data. Examples include methods based on Markov Chain Monte Carlo \cite{farooq2013} and Bayesian-network sampling \cite{zhou_creating_2022}. A persistent limitation, however, is that the resulting spatial non-stationarity remains unresolved because the same set of parameters or the same structural template is used throughout the synthesis process.

Recently, generative models have provided a new possibility for recovering the correct copula structure while addressing spatial non-stationarity in synthetic populations \cite{ang2025,sane2025}. Among them, diffusion models are particularly well suited to this task because they can learn the joint distribution through an iterative denoising process, without requiring a predefined copula template or hand-specified parameter settings \cite{ho2020}. Conditional diffusion is especially relevant because it can use regional demographic summaries as constraints while allowing the dependence structure itself to vary across space. 

The goal of this work is to propose a generative framework using a diffusion model to generate the synthetic population. 

In this paper, we therefore ask whether a diffusion model trained on PUMA-level distributions can recover region-specific copulas more accurately than methods that rely on a single national seed.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Overview}
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, our goal is to reconstruct synthetic populations whose four core attributes follow observed regional joint distributions derived from public-use microdata, while preserving realistic spatial heterogeneity through region-specific copulas; earnings are then assigned conditionally to obtain five-attribute synthetic individuals. The framework comprises three stages, summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:framework}. First, for each of the 2,456 Public Use Microdata Areas in the United States, we construct PUMS-derived regional targets together with external census conditions describing age, sex, education, employment, and earnings-related summaries. Second, a conditional denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) is trained to reconstruct the regional joint distribution from these conditions, with the conditioning signal encoded as a regional context. Lastly, the trained model generates a region-specific four-attribute joint distribution for each PUMA, which is then used to sample synthetic persons and assign earnings conditionally, producing five-attribute synthetic records.

\subsection{Data Sources and Study Area}\label{sec:data_and_study_area}

This framework uses two complementary data sources. The first is the 2023 five-year American Community Survey (i.e., ACS) Public Use Microdata Sample (i.e., PUMS), covering 2,456 Public Use Microdata Areas (i.e., PUMAs) in the United States \cite{acs, pums}. PUMS provides person-level microdata and survey weights, which we use to construct region-specific training targets. The rationale is that PUMS is an individual-level dataset representing approximately 5\% of the real population, so the copula structure and spatial heterogeneity can be preserved when each PUMA is represented by an empirical regional joint distribution rather than by marginal frequencies alone.

The second data source is the published ACS summary tables. In this framework, PUMS is used to construct the regional training targets, whereas the published ACS summary tables provide the conditioning inputs used during both training and inference. Specifically, we use B01001 for age and sex, B15003 for educational attainment, B23025 for employment status, and B20001 for earnings. These tables are selected because they are publicly available, defined on standard census categories, and match the type of regional information that would be observable at deployment.

The final synthetic population contains five person-level attributes. The regional distribution jointly models four core attributes (age, sex, education, and employment). Earnings are then assigned conditionally after the four-attribute synthetic persons are sampled. We use this design because the first four attributes have the cleanest shared conditioning schema across PUMS and ACS summary tables, while earnings enter through a separate but aligned external summary (B20001) and a person-level target constructed from PUMS earnings records (Table~\ref{tab:var_attr}).

To align the two data sources, we map person-level records to the categorical support used by the ACS summary tables. Age is discretized into census-aligned bins. Education and employment are represented with operational categories that preserve all-population coverage. For example, the education variable includes a \texttt{not\_25p} category for people below the ACS education universe, and the employment variable includes a \texttt{not\_16p} category for people below the ACS employment universe. Earnings are represented as EARN\_16p\_bin, a six-category variable constructed from ACS table B20001 and the PUMS earnings measure PERNP. People younger than 16 or with no reported earnings are assigned to \texttt{not\_in\_earnings\_universe}; the remaining records are grouped into \$0--25k, \$25k--50k, \$50k--75k, \$75k--100k, and \$100k+ bins. This preprocessing ensures that the regional targets and the external conditions live on the same discrete state space.

For out-of-sample spatial evaluation, we use Michigan as the study area and hold it out from nationwide training. Michigan contains 68 test PUMAs and spans metropolitan, suburban, and rural contexts. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneity} and Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:S2_heterogeneity}, its heterogeneity values lie well within the national range rather than forming an extreme outlier, making it a suitable held-out state for evaluating whether the model recovers region-specific dependence rather than a national-average copula.

\subsection{Constructing Regional Targets and External Conditions}
\label{sec:training_target}

Step 1 produces two region-level objects for each PUMA: a PUMS-derived training target and an external condition vector built from published ACS summary tables. The training target is the four-attribute regional joint distribution over age, sex, education, and employment. The condition vector contains the observable regional summaries for age, sex, education, employment, and earnings. Across the United States, this yields 2,456 aligned region-level examples.

We construct the target joint vector $\mathbf{p}$ from survey-weighted joint cell counts. To avoid zero-probability cells in sparse tables, we add a Laplace pseudo-count ($\alpha=1$) to each cell and then normalize the table to sum to one. The resulting vector $\mathbf{p}$ is the empirical four-attribute regional joint distribution used for training.

For the same PUMA, we construct an external condition vector $\mathbf{c}$ by concatenating the normalized ACS summary blocks for age, sex, education, employment, and earnings. Each block is normalized to sum to one within variable, so $\mathbf{c}$ records the regional composition implied by the published census summaries rather than the raw population size. The target vector $\mathbf{p}$ and the condition vector $\mathbf{c}$ therefore share the same geography and the same categorical support, while playing different roles in the model: $\mathbf{p}$ provides the supervision target, and $\mathbf{c}$ provides the observable conditioning input.

We then map each probability vector to log-probability space before training:
\[
  \mathbf{x}_0 = \log(\mathbf{p}+\phi), \quad \phi=10^{-6}.
\]

This transformation compresses the gap between common and rare cells and makes the continuous diffusion target numerically more stable.

We further standardize the log-probability vectors within each training fold:
\[
  \mathbf{z}_0 = \frac{\mathbf{x}_0 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{train}}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{train}}},
\]
where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{train}}$ and $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{train}}$ are computed from the training split only, and a small-variance floor of $10^{-6}$ is applied to $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{train}}$ for numerical stability. This fold-specific normalization centers and rescales the target vectors without leaking information from held-out regions.

\subsection{Training the Regional-Context Diffusion Model}
\label{sec:training_model}
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, the central challenge is to recover a PUMA-specific joint distribution from published regional summaries rather than from the joint distribution itself. In the current framework, the model receives external census conditions and must reconstruct the four-attribute regional joint distribution over age, sex, education, and employment. We therefore train a conditional denoising diffusion model in which the raw external condition vector is first encoded into a \emph{regional context} vector $\mathbf{r}$. This regional context summarizes the age, sex, education, employment, and earnings-related signals available for a target PUMA and is provided to the diffusion generator throughout denoising.

The main diffusion branch generates the full 500-cell joint distribution defined by the fine age, sex, education, and employment categories. To stabilize this high-dimensional state space, we add a coarse auxiliary branch that uses the same regional context $\mathbf{r}$ to predict a 72-cell coarse joint distribution, obtained by merging the fine age, education, and employment categories into the coarser schema used in our mechanism analysis. During training, the fine distribution implied by the diffusion prediction is aggregated to the same 72-cell space and penalized when it disagrees with the coarse auxiliary prediction. This auxiliary branch is used to anchor the full generator to large-scale regional structure; the final synthetic population is still generated from the full four-attribute diffusion model.

The diffusion component follows the standard noise-prediction objective. The denoiser $\epsilon_\theta$ is implemented as a two-hidden-layer multilayer perceptron with hidden dimensions [512, 512], sigmoid linear unit (SiLU) activations, and a 128-dimensional sinusoidal timestep embedding. Conditioning is injected by concatenating the regional context $\mathbf{r}$ with the noised input $\mathbf{z}_t$ and the timestep embedding. Let $\mathbf{z}_0$ denote the standardized log-probability vector defined in Section~\ref{sec:training_target}. The diffusion loss is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}=
  \mathbb{E}_{t,\,\mathbf{z}_0,\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
  \bigl[\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,t,\mathbf{r})\|^2\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{z}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, and $\bar\alpha_t$ follows a linear noise schedule with $\beta_1=10^{-4}$ and $\beta_T=0.02$ over $T=200$ timesteps.

We train the full model with a combined objective,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}
  =
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}
  +
  \lambda_{\mathrm{coarse}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coarse}}
  +
  \lambda_{\mathrm{cons}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coarse}}$ is the cross-entropy loss for the 72-cell coarse target and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}$ is the total-variation penalty between the coarse auxiliary prediction and the coarse distribution obtained by aggregating the fine diffusion prediction. In the experiments reported below, we use $\lambda_{\mathrm{coarse}}=0.5$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{cons}}=1.0$. Optimization uses AdamW (learning rate $=10^{-3}$, weight decay $=10^{-4}$), gradient clipping at 1.0, and batch size 512. To avoid relying on the last checkpoint by default, we maintain an exponential moving average of model weights (decay $=0.999$), evaluate every 200 epochs on a held-out validation split drawn from the training geographies, and select the checkpoint with the lowest validation TVD for final testing.

\subsection{Inferring the joint distribution}
\label{sec:inference}

At inference, the trained model maps a target region's condition vector $\mathbf{c}$ to a region-specific joint distribution. For an unseen region, the generation pipeline proceeds as follows: (1)~draw initial noise $\mathbf{z}_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$; (2)~run the DDPM reverse denoising process conditioned on $\mathbf{c}$ to obtain $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_0$; (3)~apply the inverse z-score transformation to recover $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$; (4)~apply softmax to obtain the raw joint probability estimate $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{\text{raw}}$; and (5)~average 128 independent denoising draws to reduce noise.

When downstream applications require discrete synthetic individuals, the resulting probability vector can be instantiated using the region's reported population count $N$, for example by converting expected cell masses into integer cell counts and sampling records from the resulting frequencies.

\subsection{Experimental design and evaluation}
\label{sec:experiment}

Conditioning signals $\mathbf{c}$ are designed to reflect the information available for a target region at inference time: aggregated census
summaries rather than the full joint distribution. In the experiments, these signals are computed by marginalizing the observed regional joint distributions from ACS PUMS so that the conditioning inputs have exact correspondence to the training targets while still representing the low-dimensional information available in practice.

We inject $\mathbf{c}$ by concatenation at the denoiser input \cite{ho2022cfg}. To isolate the contribution of different constraint types, we evaluate four conditioning modes. The dimension of the condition vector depends on the bin structure of the target joint table. Using the 5-variable coarse setting as an example, with $B=(2,2,2,2,2)$ and $K=32$, the four modes are defined as follows:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{none} ($d_c=0$): no external demographic constraint.
  \item \textbf{marginal} ($d_c=\textstyle\sum_d B_d=10$): concatenated one-dimensional marginals for the five attributes.
  \item \textbf{pairwise} ($d_c=\textstyle\sum_{i<j}B_iB_j=40$): concatenated two-dimensional cross-tabulations for all $\binom{D}{2}$ variable pairs.
  \item \textbf{marg+pair} ($d_c=50$): the marginal and pairwise signals concatenated into a single condition vector.
\end{itemize}

In this setting, pairwise conditions add explicit second-order association information beyond one-dimensional marginals. Together, these four modes form an ablation ladder in which each step adds a specific layer of demographic information, allowing us to compare the incremental value of marginals and pairwise cross-tabulations for dependence recovery.

We evaluate reconstruction accuracy with TVD \cite{lovelace2015},
\[
  \text{TVD} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_k|\hat{p}_k-p_k| ,
\]
where $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ denotes the generated joint distribution and $\mathbf{p}$ denotes the observed ground-truth distribution for a given PUMA. Lower TVD indicates closer agreement between generated and observed regional joint distributions.

We also report an independence baseline constructed as the outer product of target marginals, which removes all dependence among attributes. Our main benchmark is IPF with a global seed, in which the population-weighted average joint distribution across training PUMAs serves as the initial table. We use IPF as the primary benchmark because it operates on the same aggregated constraints and the same distribution-level target as the diffusion pipeline. By contrast, combinatorial-optimization approaches require an explicit pool of individual records and solve a different synthesis problem, so they are not directly comparable in the present setting. For fair comparison, both diffusion outputs and the global-seed IPF benchmark undergo the same final marginal calibration, implemented with IPF, to enforce exact target marginals. Under this protocol, performance differences can be attributed to seed quality rather than to marginal adjustment.

We further assess geographic generalizability with a 5-fold cross-validation over Michigan's 68 PUMAs, partitioned into test folds of 13--14 regions. In each round, one Michigan fold is used for testing, while the remaining four Michigan folds together with all non-Michigan PUMAs are used for training. Fold-level differences between diffusion and IPF are summarized with a one-sided sign test \cite{demsar2006}; under the null hypothesis that the two methods are equally likely to outperform one another in any given fold, the probability that one method wins all five folds by chance is $(1/2)^5 = 0.031$.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Generated joint distributions capture region-specific dependence}

To make the implications of regional heterogeneity concrete, we examine generated outputs for two representative Michigan PUMAs. The case studies are the Troy--Rochester PUMA (2602903) and the Ionia--Montcalm--Mecosta--Osceola PUMA (2601100). For visualization, each regional joint distribution is converted to expected person counts across the $K=32$ demographic cells using the reported regional population total.

The two regions differ markedly, with a between-region joint TVD of 0.253. This gap is driven primarily by socioeconomic composition rather than basic demographics: age and sex differ only modestly (TVDs of 0.015 and 0.023, respectively), whereas income and education diverge much more strongly. In PUMA~2602903, 63.4\% of the population falls in the high-education category, and substantial mass appears in both the ``high-income, high-education'' and ``low-income, low-education'' cells (34.9\% and 34.0\%, respectively). In PUMA~2601100, by contrast, mass is much more concentrated in the ``low-income, low-education'' cell (54.1\%), with only 12.8\% in the highest socioeconomic cell.

Figure~\ref{fig:output_examples} shows reconstruction quality at three levels. At the one-dimensional level, the generated marginal distributions for age, sex, income, education, and employment align exactly with the target marginals (Fig.~\ref{fig:output_examples}a,d). The remaining discrepancies in panels~b--f therefore reflect dependence recovery rather than marginal mismatch.

At the pairwise level, we assess the two-dimensional education$\times$income cross-tabulation, which captures a local dependence pattern in these examples. This pairwise contrast is recovered with high accuracy in both regions: in each PUMA, the generated cross-tabulation remains extremely close to its observed counterpart, with TVDs of $1.9\times10^{-4}$ for PUMA~2602903 and $7.5\times10^{-4}$ for PUMA~2601100 (Fig.~\ref{fig:output_examples}b,e). 

At the full-joint level, the task becomes more stringent because all five attributes must be recovered simultaneously (Fig.~\ref{fig:output_examples}c,f). Relative to the pairwise projection, the error therefore increases, but the resulting full-joint TVDs remain low: 0.0209 for PUMA~2602903 and 0.0153 for PUMA~2601100. This gap relative to the cross-tab errors indicates that the dominant two-dimensional dependence pattern is recovered more tightly than the full multivariate structure, with the remaining error concentrated in higher-order interactions. These values nevertheless remain close to the 68-region test-set mean TVD of 0.024 reported for the full held-out evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:cond32}).

\subsection{Verification and validation}
\label{sec:verification}

Raw generated outputs prior to final marginal calibration (Fig.~\ref{fig:verification}a) show how closely the model already respects the target marginals. Under pairwise conditioning at $K=32$, the raw marginal TVD remains below 0.002 for all five attributes, ranging from 0.0009 for sex to 0.0015 for employment. The diffusion model therefore produces distributions that are already close to the target marginals.

Training convergence and sampling stability provide two additional checks, both evaluated with the final calibrated joint TVD to match the main benchmarking setup. Training converges quickly (Fig.~\ref{fig:verification}b): the mean joint TVD falls from 0.044 at epoch 100 to 0.0256 by epoch 3,000, reaches 0.0236 at epoch 10,000, and changes only slightly thereafter (0.0238 at epoch 30,000). By epoch 3,000, the model has already captured about 90\% of the total improvement observed by epoch 10,000, and extending training to 30,000 changes the mean TVD by only 0.0002.

Sampling stability shows a similar pattern (Fig.~\ref{fig:verification}c). Averaging 16 denoising draws reduces the mean TVD from 0.0266 for a single draw to 0.0239, and the curve is effectively flat beyond 64 draws. At 128 draws, the standard deviation across 10 random seeds is only $2.6\times10^{-5}$, indicating that run-to-run variation is negligible at the sampling budget used in the main experiments.

\subsection{Constraint informativeness drives dependence recovery}

While the preceding results show that the model can generate accurate and generalizable joint distributions, they do not yet identify which information sources drive dependence recovery. We address this question with a controlled ablation study across four conditioning modes: no constraints, one-dimensional marginals only, two-dimensional pairwise cross-tabulations only, and a combination of both. The resulting TVD is then measured against the true joint distribution.

%edit from here

For the 5-variable coarse setting ($K=32$), the effect of conditioning information is clear (Table~\ref{tab:cond32}; Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}a). Without any local constraints ($d_c=0$), the diffusion model still learns a nontrivial prior over regional joint distributions, achieving a TVD of 0.114, substantially below the independence reference of 0.290. Adding one-dimensional marginals ($d_c=10$) reduces the TVD to 0.043, showing that local frequency information already removes much of the error. Replacing marginal-only conditioning with pairwise cross-tabulations ($d_c=40$) further reduces the TVD to 0.024, a 44\% improvement relative to the marginal baseline. Adding marginals on top of pairwise constraints produces almost no further gain (0.0239 versus 0.0240), suggesting that in this setting the pairwise tables already contain most of the first-order information needed for accurate recovery.

This pattern can be understood in terms of what each conditioning mode contributes. One-dimensional marginals constrain how common each category is, but they do not specify how attributes co-occur. For example, knowing only the marginal shares of high income and high education does not distinguish a region where these two attributes concentrate in the same people from one where they are distributed across different people. Pairwise cross-tabulations add exactly this missing information by specifying how attribute pairs co-vary. As a result, pairwise conditioning constrains the joint distribution much more tightly than marginals alone.

The same interpretation extends beyond the coarse $K=32$ setting (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}b). In the 5-variable $K=128$ setting, replacing marginals with pairwise conditioning reduces the TVD from 0.070 to 0.051, a 26\% improvement over the marginal baseline. In the 3-variable fine setting ($K=256$), adding pairwise information reduces the TVD from 0.101 to 0.084, a 17\% improvement. Across the reported person-level settings, the pairwise gain is 17\% in the 3-variable setting and 26--44\% in the 5-variable settings. Taken together, these results show that performance improves most when the conditioning inputs specify how attributes co-vary, not just how common each category is. The remaining error is consistent with structure above the pairwise level, which these inputs do not specify directly.

\subsection{Comparison with iterative proportional fitting}

We compare diffusion against an IPF baseline initialized from a global, national-average seed. In the IPF baseline, the seed is the population-weighted average joint distribution across training PUMAs. In the diffusion pipeline, the seed is a region-specific joint distribution generated from local constraints. Because both pipelines apply the same final marginal calibration, implemented with IPF, any remaining performance difference reflects seed quality rather than the calibration step itself.

Across all four reported person-level configurations, diffusion produces lower TVD than the global-seed IPF baseline, with relative reductions ranging from 19\% to 59\% (Table~\ref{tab:main}; Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison}a,d). The largest improvement appears in the 5-variable coarse setting ($K=32$), where pairwise-conditioned diffusion reaches a TVD of 0.024 compared with 0.058 for IPF. The advantage persists at finer resolutions of the same task, with TVD decreasing from 0.081 to 0.051 at $K=128$, and it remains present in the finer 3-variable setting, where diffusion reaches 0.084 versus 0.103 at $K=256$ and 0.055 versus 0.070 at $K=64$.

The same advantage remains under the Michigan 5-fold cross-validation protocol (Table~\ref{tab:mi5fold}; Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison}b). Across five folds over Michigan's 68 PUMAs, diffusion outperforms IPF in every case (one-sided sign test $p=0.031$), with a mean TVD difference of $-0.014 \pm 0.002$. The result therefore does not depend on a single train-test split within Michigan.

\section{Discussion}

The condition ablation reveals a structural insight about synthesis as an inverse problem: the critical resource is not model capacity but constraint informativeness. From none to marginal to pairwise, each additional layer of constraint information reduces error by a large, discrete step. Model architecture matters only at the margin. The advantage reverses at $K=512$, where two mechanisms jointly prevent recovery: cell sparsity ($\sim$14 persons per cell vs.\ $\sim$220 at $K=32$) and constraint insufficiency (128 pairwise parameters against 511 joint unknowns), so that even extending training from 10k to 30k epochs does not close the gap (Supplementary Table~S1, Fig.~S1). This reframes the scaling boundary not as a model failure but as an information-theoretic limit: enriching input constraints is more effective than increasing model capacity or training budget.

The choice of training target---predicting regional distributions rather than individual records---has implications beyond this specific model. Tabular generative models trained on individual samples \cite{kotelnikov2023,stoian2024} optimize per-record quality, which does not guarantee population-level distributional accuracy---a tension between feasibility and diversity that recent work has begun to formalize \cite{kim2022}. For synthesis tasks evaluated on regional joint statistics---the standard in transportation and urban modeling---training directly on the aggregate quantity of interest aligns the learning objective with the evaluation metric, and our results suggest this alignment is decisive.

The dependence heterogeneity finding (mean TVD 0.19 across 2,456 regions) has independent value and provides a quantitative frame for interpreting model performance. The 0.19 TVD represents the average deviation of regional dependence from the global seed that IPF uses. At $K=32$, pairwise-conditioned diffusion reduces TVD from 0.058 (IPF) to 0.024---closing approximately 60\% of this gap. At $K=128$, it closes about 37\%. These numbers give a concrete answer to ``what did the model learn'': it recovered a substantial fraction of the spatial dependence variation that IPF's global seed ignores. This motivates not only learned approaches but also stratified IPF schemes that use cluster-specific seeds.

Limitations are explicit. First, experiments use United States PUMS; generalization to other countries depends on comparable marginal systems. Second, pairwise conditions require cross-tabulations that may not always be publicly available, though they can be estimated from microdata samples. Third, the evaluated person-level settings still use relatively coarse discretizations, so performance at finer resolutions remains constrained by sparsity and limited higher-order information. Fourth, the current model does not enforce marginal constraints architecturally---this is handled by a final marginal calibration step, implemented with IPF, which adds a processing step.

A concrete path forward: enrich constraints before enlarging models. Higher-order cross-tabulations are ideal but often unavailable. Tract-level auxiliary descriptors---built environment, labor structure, and university/industrial contexts---offer a feasible route to increase conditional identifiability in high-dimensional settings. More broadly, population synthesis can be viewed as an instance of ecological inference \cite{king1997}---reconstructing individual-level structure from aggregate observations---for which distribution-level generative models provide a natural probabilistic language. Extending the present framework to sub-regional disaggregation, where building-level spatial priors replace marginal constraints as the conditioning signal, is an immediate next step enabled by increasingly available building footprint and land-use data \cite{rineer2025}.

\subsection*{S1. Dimensional scaling and the information-sparsity boundary}

All five configurations with $K\leq256$ show diffusion advantage over IPF. Testing whether this holds at higher dimensionality, we train on $K=512$ (fine-grained 5-variable joint: $4\times2\times4\times4\times4$ bins). Diffusion TVD reaches 0.139 while IPF achieves 0.112, a reversal of $+0.028$ (Supplementary Table~S1).

Two mechanisms drive this reversal. First, \textit{cell sparsity}: at $K=32$, each joint cell contains $\sim$220 persons on average; at $K=512$, only $\sim$14, making empirical probability estimates unreliable as training targets. Second, \textit{constraint insufficiency}: the pairwise condition vector provides 128 parameters, but the $K=512$ joint has 511 free parameters---a coverage ratio of 25\%, versus 129\% at $K=32$. Extending training from 10k to 30k epochs reduces TVD from 0.182 to 0.147 ($-$19\%) but does not reach IPF's 0.112, confirming the bottleneck is information, not optimization. A counterintuitive corollary: pairwise conditioning at $K=512$ (TVD = 0.249) is \textit{worse} than marginal-only (0.147), because the 128-dim pairwise input must reconstruct 511 unknowns without the global-prior support that IPF supplies to the marginal-only pipeline.
