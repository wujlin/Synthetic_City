\begin{abstract}
Reconstructing how demographic attributes co-occur within a region---not just their individual frequencies---is a central challenge in population synthesis. The standard method, iterative proportional fitting (IPF), fills this gap by reusing a fixed national-average template, implicitly assuming that the dependence structure is the same everywhere. We show empirically that this assumption is violated: regional dependence patterns vary substantially across more than 2,400 regions in the United States. To learn these region-specific patterns, we train a conditional diffusion model to directly predict the quantity that matters---the regional joint probability vector---treating each region's observed distribution as a single training example. This approach consistently outperforms IPF across five experimental configurations (up to 59\% error reduction), with statistical significance confirmed in a five-fold spatial cross-validation. Systematic ablations reveal that performance is driven by the quality of input constraints---two-variable cross-tabulations provide up to 44\% additional improvement over one-variable summaries---establishing constraint informativeness, not model capacity, as the critical resource for dependence recovery.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The creation of a synthetic population by reconstructing the joint distribution of demographic attributes within regions from aggregated data (i.e., data reporting regional marginal distributions of individual attributes) has long been regarded as a foundation of urban simulation, transportation modeling, and policy analysis \cite{mueller2011,barthelemy2013,la2025,chapuis2022}. However, aggregated data drastically under-specify the full picture from a statistical perspective. For example, when creating a synthetic population with five attributes and four values per attribute, the joint distribution contains more than one thousand potential combinations, whereas current methods typically rely on one-dimensional marginals alone. As a result, the underlying dependence structure---that is, the copula \cite{sklar1959} describing how multiple attributes co-occur---may be missed. The resulting synthetic population can therefore contain internal bias, such as assigning an impossible combination of attributes to a single synthetic individual.

Recovering a joint distribution from marginals alone is inherently difficult because the copula is hard to reconstruct \cite{frogner2019}. Existing population-synthesis methods nevertheless attempt to address this problem through two broad strategies: synthetic reconstruction and combinatorial optimization \cite{chapuis2022}. In the synthetic-reconstruction family, methods such as Hierarchical Sampling (HS) \cite{jiang_large-scale_2024}, Iterative Proportional Fitting (IPF) \cite{predhumeau_synthetic_2023}, and Iterative Proportional Updating (IPU) \cite{ipu2009} recover joint distributions from aggregated data (e.g., census-tract-level data) by matching target marginals while preserving the seed copula structure \cite{choupani2016,lovelace2015}. In the combinatorial-optimization family \cite{harland2012}, one extends real individual-level microdata or adjusts existing synthetic individuals to match target joint distributions through fitness evaluation and optimized sampling procedures.

However, these methods share a common limitation: the copula structure is extracted from aggregated data or real individual-level microdata and then applied uniformly across regions. This works when regions are demographically similar, but when regions are heterogeneous, a fixed copula can misrepresent local dependence and introduce internal bias into the resulting synthetic population \cite{roxburgh2025}. A related issue is spatial non-stationarity \cite{krupskii2018,mondal2024,bastin2023}. In the United States, for example, dependence structures vary substantially across the 2,456 Public Use Microdata Areas (PUMAs). We quantify this variation by comparing each regional dependence structure with the national average and obtain a mean total variation distance (TVD) of 0.19, which is far from negligible and far from uniform across regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:heterogeneity}). In practical terms, a university region with a distinctive ``young + low-income + high-education'' profile does not share the same age--income--education dependence pattern as a manufacturing region.

To handle such copula structures, optimization methods such as integer least squares programming have been used to create synthetic populations for the United States from Census Block Group data \cite{lin2023}. Parametric approaches instead use statistical inference to learn the joint relationship among hidden attributes in the data. Examples include methods based on Markov Chain Monte Carlo \cite{farooq2013} and Bayesian-network sampling \cite{zhou_creating_2022}. A persistent limitation, however, is that the resulting spatial non-stationarity remains unresolved because the same set of parameters or the same structural template is used throughout the synthesis process.

Recently, generative models have provided a new possibility for recovering the correct copula structure while addressing spatial non-stationarity in synthetic populations \cite{ang2025,sane2025}. Among them, diffusion models are particularly well suited to this task because they can learn the joint distribution through an iterative denoising process, without requiring a predefined copula template or hand-specified parameter settings \cite{ho2020}. Conditional diffusion is especially relevant because it can use regional demographic summaries as constraints while allowing the dependence structure itself to vary across space. In this paper, we therefore ask whether a diffusion model trained on PUMA-level distributions can recover region-specific copulas more accurately than methods that rely on a single national seed.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Overview}
%The goal of this work is to learn region-specific dependence structures from data, replacing the fixed global template used by standard methods. 
The primary objective of this framework is to accurately reconstruct region-specific joint demographic distributions from low-dimensional aggregate data, overcoming the assumption of spatial homogeneity inherent in standard population synthesis methods. 

Recovering $\mathbf{p}$ from marginals alone is fundamentally underdetermined: $K-1$ unknowns versus $\sum_d(B_d-1)$ constraints. Traditional IPF resolves this by computing the information projection (I-projection) of a seed $\mathbf{q}$ onto the marginal-consistent polytope \cite{csiszar1975}, preserving the seed's interaction structure. However, when $\mathbf{q}$ is the population-weighted national average, every region inherits the same copula. Our diffusion model replaces this fixed $\mathbf{q}$ with a learned, region-specific estimate through three conceptual stages (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}):


%The Introduction identifies two challenges: (1)~regional copulas vary substantially across space, and (2)~recovering a high-dimensional joint distribution from low-dimensional marginal constraints is fundamentally underdetermined. Our approach addresses both through a three-stage pipeline (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}).

\textbf{Stage~1: Distribution-level representation.}
For each of 2,456 regions in the United States (PUMAs), we construct the empirical joint probability vector $\mathbf{p}\in\Delta^{K-1}$ over $D$ discretized demographic attributes, where $K=\prod_{d=1}^{D}B_d$ is the number of joint cells. We treat each region's $\mathbf{p}$ as a single training example, directly aligning the learning objective with the evaluation metric (\S\ref{sec:training_target}).

\textbf{Stage~2: Conditional diffusion learning.}
A denoising diffusion model learns to map low-dimensional demographic constraints (specifically, one-dimensional marginals and two-dimensional cross-tabulations) to the full joint distribution.
The model is trained under the standard denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) $\epsilon$-prediction objective, with constraints injected via concatenation at the denoiser input. Four conditioning modes (none, marginal, pairwise, marg+pair) form a controlled ablation ladder that isolates the information contribution of each constraint type (\S\ref{sec:condition_design}).


\textbf{Stage~3: Post-processing and evaluation.}
At inference, an ensemble of independent denoising draws is averaged and passed through softmax to produce a raw probability estimate; one round of post-hoc IPF then enforces exact marginal consistency. The same post-processing is applied to the global-seed IPF baseline, so that any performance difference reflects seed quality alone. Evaluation uses a leave-Michigan-out spatial split to prevent autocorrelation leakage (\S\ref{sec:evaluation}).

A critical challenge in high-dimensional configurations (e.g., $K=512$) is cell sparsity, which can produce zero-count cells and result in numerically undefined diffusion targets ($\log(0)=-\infty$). To ensure the log-probability vector remains well-defined throughout, we apply Laplace smoothing ($\alpha=1$) to the joint cell counts, adding one pseudo-count per cell prior to probability normalization. This operation provides a necessary mathematical floor of approximately $log(\frac{1}{N+K})\approx-9$ for sparse cells. Crucially, this perturbation is negligible in low-dimensional settings, altering cell probabilities by $<0.5\%$ at $K=32$.

\label{sec:training_target}

To directly align the learning objective with our population-level evaluation metric, we define the training unit at the distribution level. Specifically, we treat each PUMA's empirical joint probability vector $\mathbf{p}$ as a single training example, yielding exactly 2,456 vectorized instances. This macroscopic approach provides a robust gradient signal for the dependence structure, overcoming the limitations of training on individual person records, which merely optimizes per-record likelihoods.

\paragraph{Log-probability representation.}
Each probability vector is mapped to log-probability space before training:
\[
  \mathbf{x}_0 = \log(\mathbf{p}+\epsilon), \quad \epsilon=10^{-6}.
\]

This logarithmic transformation serves a dual purpose. First, it compresses the dynamic range of near-zero cells that would otherwise disproportionately dominate the reconstruction error in absolute probability space. Second, it ensures the resulting distribution over $\mathbf{x}_0$ is approximately Gaussian, satisfying a fundamental prerequisite for the DDPM noise schedule to operate correctly.

%This representation compresses the dynamic range of near-zero cells, which would dominate reconstruction error in absolute probability space. And it produces a distribution over $\mathbf{x}_0$ that is approximately Gaussian (i.e., a prerequisite for the DDPM noise schedule to operate correctly).

\paragraph{Z-score normalization.}
%The log-probability vector is further standardized per training fold:
In its raw logarithmic form, the distribution of $\mathbf{x}_0$ is strongly shifted (e.g., mean $\approx-11$ and standard deviation $\approx4$ at $K=512$). This shift places the training targets far outside the standard normal $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ regime assumed by the linear noise schedule. To correct this, the log-probability vector is strictly standardized per training fold:
\[
  \mathbf{z}_0 = \frac{\mathbf{x}_0 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{train}}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{train}}},
\]
where a small-variance floor of $10^{-6}$ is applied to $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{train}}$ to ensure numerical stability. Empirically, this z-score normalization is indispensable; omitting it consistently caused the generative process to collapse (yielding a near-random TVD of $\approx0.98$), as the unnormalized target distribution violates the fundamental assumptions of the linear noise schedule.


%with a small-variance floor of $10^{-6}$ applied to $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{train}}$. Empirically, omitting z-score normalization collapses TVD to near-random ($\approx0.98$); with it, TVD recovers to competitive levels across all $K$ settings tested.

\subsection{Condition design}
\label{sec:condition_design}

To bridge the gap between the generative model and real-world data availability, we design conditioning signals ($\mathbf{c}$) that guide the diffusion process. These signals precisely encode the demographic constraints available to a practitioner at test time, representing aggregate statistics that can be derived from published census tables. 

These conditions are injected by concatenating $\mathbf{c}$ with the denoiser input \cite{ho2022cfg}. To systematically isolate the information contribution of different constraint types, we evaluate four distinct conditioning modes. For clarity, the input dimensions ($d_c$) are illustrated below using the 5-variable coarse setting (bin structure $B=(2,2,2,2,2)$, $K=32$):

%Conditions encode the demographic constraints available at test time---statistics that a practitioner can derive from published aggregate data without access to microdata. 

%Conditions are injected by concatenating $\mathbf{c}$ with the denoiser input \cite{ho2022cfg}. Four modes are tested; dimensions are given for the 5-var coarse setting with bin structure $B=(2,2,2,2,2)$, $K=32$:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{none} ($d_c=0$): Unconditional generation, which tests the model's learned prior over plausible copulas.
  \item \textbf{marginal} ($d_c=\textstyle\sum_d B_d=10$): Concatenated one-dimensional marginals that encode each attribute's frequency independently (readily available from standard census marginal tables).
  \item \textbf{pairwise} ($d_c=\textstyle\sum_{i<j}B_iB_j=40$): Concatenated two-dimensional cross-tabulations for all $\binom{D}{2}$ variable pairs. These directly encode second-order associations, providing the explicit structural information that a global-seed IPF inherently lacks.
  \item \textbf{marg+pair} ($d_c=50$): Both marginal and pairwise signals concatenated.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Sampling and post-processing}

To instantiate the synthetic population for an unseen target region, the generation pipeline translates the learned conditions into a discrete probability distribution. Specifically, the process proceeds as follows: (1)~draw initial noise $\mathbf{z}_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$; (2)~run the DDPM reverse denoising process conditioned on the region's specific vector $\mathbf{c}$ to yield $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_0$; (3)~apply the inverse z-score transformation to recover $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$; (4)~apply the softmax function to generate the raw probability estimate $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{\text{raw}}$; and (5)~average 128 independent denoising draws to mitigate sampling noise and produce a robust final estimate.

While the diffusion model effectively captures the regional dependence structure, standard population synthesis requires absolute fidelity to known macro-level marginals. To enforce this agreement, we apply a single round of post-hoc IPF to the raw estimates.Crucially, this identical calibration is applied to the global-seed IPF baseline.By ensuring both pipelines are adjusted to match target marginals, this experimental design strictly isolates the seed quality (the accuracy of the underlying dependence struture) as the sole driver of any performance difference.

Given the region's census-reported population count $N$, the calibrated probability vector $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ assigns $n_k=\mathrm{Round}(N\times\hat{p}_k)$ individuals to each demographic cell $k$.This procedure guarantees a final  synthetic population that faithfully reproduces both the target marginal frequencies and the localized dependence structure.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:evaluation}

To rigorously evaluate the model's capacity to generalize across diverse geographic contexts, we adopt a strictly spatial leave-out evaluation framework. Unlike conventional random sampling, which often inflates apparent performance due to spatial autocorrelation leakage (e.g., neighboring regions sharing labor markets or commuting zones appearing in both the training and test sets), holding out an entire state ensures structural independence. Specifically, Michigan's 68 PUMAs form the isolated test set, while the remaining 2,388 PUMAs serve as the training set. For the 2-variable household configuration, we further partition the held-out Michigan regions into five folds of 13--14 PUMAs. This nested structure enables robust fold-level statistical validation using a one-sided sign test \cite{demsar2006}: under the null hypothesis that the diffusion model and the traditional IPF are equally likely to outperform one another in any given fold, the probability of the diffusion model winning all five folds strictly by chance is constrained to $(1/2)^5 = 0.031$.

To quantify the fidelity of the generated synthetic populations, our primary evaluation metric is the Total Variation Distance (TVD)[cite: 113], defined mathematically as:
$$
  \text{TVD} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_k|\hat{p}_k-p_k| ,
$$
where $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ represents the generated joint distribution and $\mathbf{p}$ is the observed ground-truth distribution for a given PUMA. TVD ranges from 0 (indicating exact recovery) to 1 (indicating zero overlap). We contextualize model performance against two established reference baselines. The absolute upper bound of error is defined by the \textbf{Independence} baseline, which assumes zero interaction among attributes (computed as the outer product of target marginals) and yields a TVD of $\approx0.290$ at $K=32$. The primary competitive benchmark is the current standard practice, \textbf{IPF (global seed)}, wherein the population-weighted average joint distribution across all training PUMAs acts as the seed, subsequently adjusted to local target marginals via post-hoc IPF. Finally, cosine similarity is reported as an auxiliary metric to provide a complementary geometric perspective on distributional alignment.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Generated joint distributions capture region-specific dependence}

% transition missing
The model's output for each target region is a complete joint probability vector $\hat{\mathbf{p}}\in\Delta^{K-1}$, from which a synthetic population is instantiated by assigning person counts to each of $K=32$ demographic cells.

Figure~\ref{fig:output_examples} illustrates this product for two Michigan PUMAs chosen to represent high and median heterogeneity, measured by TVD-to-global in Fig.~\ref{fig:heterogeneity}: Oakland County (East Central)--Troy \& Rochester Area PUMA (2602903; near the 90th percentile) and Ionia, Montcalm, Mecosta \& Osceola Counties PUMA (2601100; near the median).

Three levels of comparison reveal how well the generated population matches reality. First, one-dimensional marginals---the age, sex, income, education, and employment composition of each region---are reproduced exactly, because post-hoc IPF enforces this by construction (Fig.~\ref{fig:output_examples}a,d). Second, and more informatively, the two-dimensional cross-tabulations capture region-specific dependence patterns. The higher-heterogeneity PUMA has an unusually high share of college-educated residents (63\%, vs.\ $\sim$40\% nationally), and this elevated education rate reshapes the income--education association: the dominant cells are ``high-education, high-income'' (35\%) and ``low-education, low-income'' (34\%), with ``high-education, low-income'' appearing at a low but non-negligible rate (2.6\%). In the median-heterogeneity comparison PUMA, by contrast, ``low-education, low-income'' dominates at 54\%. In both cases, the generated cross-tabulation closely matches the observed one (Fig.~\ref{fig:output_examples}b,e). Third, across all 32 joint cells---including tail cells where population shares fall below 1\%---generated and observed person counts track closely, with TVD of 0.021 and 0.015 respectively (Fig.~\ref{fig:output_examples}c,f). These values are consistent with the 68-region test-set average of 0.024 (Table~\ref{tab:cond32}), and the more atypical region is reconstructed as accurately as the median case.

\subsection{Verification and validation}

Under pairwise conditioning at $K=32$, the model's raw outputs---before any post-hoc IPF adjustment---already recover the correct one-dimensional marginals with TVD $<0.002$ for all five attributes (Fig.~\ref{fig:verification}a). This learned marginal consistency demonstrates that the model has internalized the structural relationship between low-order constraints and the full joint distribution: it produces outputs that are nearly marginal-consistent by construction, rather than relying on post-processing to enforce this property.

To test whether performance generalizes to unseen regions, we conduct a Michigan 5-fold cross-validation. Michigan's 68 PUMAs are split into five folds of 13--14 regions; each fold is held out in turn while the model trains on all non-Michigan regions plus the remaining Michigan folds. Across all five folds, TVD ranges from 0.065 to 0.077, with inter-fold standard deviation of 0.005. This stability indicates that the model captures systematic structure in the marginal-to-joint mapping rather than overfitting to training regions. We chose Michigan (an entire state) as the held-out unit to avoid spatial autocorrelation leakage that would arise from random regional splits.

Training convergence is rapid: TVD improves sharply during the first 3{,}000 epochs, capturing approximately 90\% of the total error reduction. Performance continues to improve slowly through epoch 10{,}000---the overall optimum---after which it stabilizes: the 30{,}000-epoch model falls within 1\% of the 10{,}000-epoch minimum (Fig.~\ref{fig:verification}b). This convergence profile suggests that the marginal-to-joint mapping is efficiently learnable and does not require extensive hyperparameter tuning of the training schedule.

Monte Carlo stability is assessed by varying the number of denoising draws averaged to produce each region's output distribution. Performance improves sharply from 1 to 16 draws as averaging reduces sampling noise, then stabilizes beyond 64 draws. At 128 draws, run-to-run standard deviation across 10 random seeds falls below 0.001 TVD (Fig.~\ref{fig:verification}c), confirming that the reported results are not sensitive to stochastic variation in the generation process.

\subsection{Constraint informativeness drives dependence recovery}

The results above establish that the model produces accurate, generalizable joint distributions, but they do not explain which types of constraints drive dependence recovery. To isolate the contribution of each information source, we perform systematic ablations: the model is trained under four conditions---no constraints, one-dimensional marginals only, two-dimensional pairwise cross-tabulations only, and both combined---and the resulting TVD is measured against the true joint.

For the 5-variable $K=32$ setting, the progression is striking (Table~\ref{tab:cond32}; Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}a): without any constraints ($d_c=0$), the diffusion model learns a prior over plausible copulas, achieving TVD = 0.114---a substantial improvement over statistical independence (0.290) but far from the true dependence. Adding one-dimensional marginals ($d_c=10$) provides local frequency information, reducing TVD to 0.043. Adding two-dimensional pairwise cross-tabulations ($d_c=40$) reduces TVD another 44\% relative to marginal-only (from 0.043 to 0.024), because these constraints directly encode second-order associations that cannot be inferred from univariate information alone.

Why is this progression meaningful? In inverse problems, the fundamental constraint is information: you cannot recover more unknowns than you have constraints. At $K=32$, the joint has 31 free parameters; one-dimensional marginals provide only 10 constraint values ($d_c=10$), leaving 21 degrees of freedom undetermined. Two-variable cross-tabulations add 40 constraint values ($d_c=40$) encoding all pairwise interactions. The model uses this richer constraint set to pin down the higher-order structure.

The model's learned marginal consistency (confirmed in \S2.2) further supports this interpretation: under pairwise conditioning, the raw outputs already satisfy the input constraints, indicating that the model has internalized the exact mapping from constraints to distribution rather than merely fitting surface-level patterns.

\section{Discussion}

The condition ablation reveals a structural insight about synthesis as an inverse problem: the critical resource is not model capacity but constraint informativeness. From none to marginal to pairwise, each additional layer of constraint information reduces error by a large, discrete step. Model architecture matters only at the margin. The advantage reverses at $K=512$, where two mechanisms jointly prevent recovery: cell sparsity ($\sim$14 persons per cell vs.\ $\sim$220 at $K=32$) and constraint insufficiency (128 pairwise parameters against 511 joint unknowns), so that even extending training from 10k to 30k epochs does not close the gap (Supplementary Table~S1, Fig.~S1). This reframes the scaling boundary not as a model failure but as an information-theoretic limit: enriching input constraints is more effective than increasing model capacity or training budget.

The choice of training target---predicting regional distributions rather than individual records---has implications beyond this specific model. Tabular generative models trained on individual samples \cite{kotelnikov2023,stoian2024} optimize per-record quality, which does not guarantee population-level distributional accuracy---a tension between feasibility and diversity that recent work has begun to formalize \cite{kim2022}. For synthesis tasks evaluated on regional joint statistics---the standard in transportation and urban modeling---training directly on the aggregate quantity of interest aligns the learning objective with the evaluation metric, and our results suggest this alignment is decisive.

The dependence heterogeneity finding (mean TVD 0.19 across 2,456 regions) has independent value and provides a quantitative frame for interpreting model performance. The 0.19 TVD represents the average deviation of regional dependence from the global seed that IPF uses. At $K=32$, pairwise-conditioned diffusion reduces TVD from 0.058 (IPF) to 0.024---closing approximately 60\% of this gap. At $K=128$, it closes about 37\%. These numbers give a concrete answer to ``what did the model learn'': it recovered a substantial fraction of the spatial dependence variation that IPF's global seed ignores. This motivates not only learned approaches but also stratified IPF schemes that use cluster-specific seeds.

Limitations are explicit. First, experiments use United States PUMS; generalization to other countries depends on comparable marginal systems. Second, pairwise conditions require cross-tabulations that may not always be publicly available, though they can be estimated from microdata samples. Third, the 2-var and 3/5-var settings use different statistical units (household vs.\ person); cross-setting comparisons serve as scaling diagnostics, not same-unit absolute rankings. Fourth, the current model does not enforce marginal constraints architecturally---this is handled by post-hoc IPF, which adds a processing step.

A concrete path forward: enrich constraints before enlarging models. Higher-order cross-tabulations are ideal but often unavailable. Tract-level auxiliary descriptors---built environment, labor structure, university/industrial profiles---offer a feasible route to increase conditional identifiability in high-dimensional settings. More broadly, population synthesis can be viewed as an instance of ecological inference \cite{king1997}---reconstructing individual-level structure from aggregate observations---for which distribution-level generative models provide a natural probabilistic language. Extending the present framework to sub-regional disaggregation, where building-level spatial priors replace marginal constraints as the conditioning signal, is an immediate next step enabled by increasingly available building footprint and land-use data \cite{rineer2025}.

\subsection*{S1. Dimensional scaling and the information-sparsity boundary}

All five configurations with $K\leq256$ show diffusion advantage over IPF. Testing whether this holds at higher dimensionality, we train on $K=512$ (fine-grained 5-variable joint: $4\times2\times4\times4\times4$ bins). Diffusion TVD reaches 0.139 while IPF achieves 0.112, a reversal of $+0.028$ (Supplementary Table~S1).

Two mechanisms drive this reversal. First, \textit{cell sparsity}: at $K=32$, each joint cell contains $\sim$220 persons on average; at $K=512$, only $\sim$14, making empirical probability estimates unreliable as training targets. Second, \textit{constraint insufficiency}: the pairwise condition vector provides 128 parameters, but the $K=512$ joint has 511 free parameters---a coverage ratio of 25\%, versus 129\% at $K=32$. Extending training from 10k to 30k epochs reduces TVD from 0.182 to 0.147 ($-$19\%) but does not reach IPF's 0.112, confirming the bottleneck is information, not optimization. A counterintuitive corollary: pairwise conditioning at $K=512$ (TVD = 0.249) is \textit{worse} than marginal-only (0.147), because the 128-dim pairwise input must reconstruct 511 unknowns without the global-prior support that IPF supplies to the marginal-only pipeline.
