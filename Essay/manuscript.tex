\title{Distribution-Level Diffusion Recovers Spatially\\Heterogeneous Dependence from Marginal Constraints}


\date{}

\begin{document}

\onehalfspacing

\begin{abstract}
Reconstructing how demographic attributes co-occur within a region---not just their individual frequencies---is a central challenge in population synthesis. The standard method, iterative proportional fitting (IPF), fills this gap by reusing a fixed national-average template, implicitly assuming that the dependence structure is the same everywhere. We show empirically that this assumption is violated: regional dependence patterns vary substantially across more than 2,400 US regions. To learn these region-specific patterns, we train a conditional diffusion model to directly predict the quantity that matters---the regional joint probability vector---treating each region's observed distribution as a single training example. This approach consistently outperforms IPF across five experimental configurations (up to 59\% error reduction), with statistical significance confirmed in a five-fold spatial cross-validation. Systematic ablations reveal that performance is driven by the quality of input constraints---two-variable cross-tabulations provide up to 44\% additional improvement over one-variable summaries---establishing constraint informativeness, not model capacity, as the critical resource for dependence recovery.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The creation of a synthetic population by reconstructing the joint distribution of demographic attributes within certain regions from aggregated-level data (i.e., data recording certain regions' marginal distributions related to individual attributes) has always been considered the foundation in urban simulation, transportation modeling, and policy analysis \cite{mueller2011,barthelemy2013,la2025,chapuis2022}. However, the core challenge is that aggregated-level data drastically under-specify the full picture from the statistical perspective. Such as, when creating the synthetic population with five attributes and each attribute has four values, the joint distribution of these attributes has over a thousand potential combinations, but current methods always use one-dimensional marginals to create these attributes, which may result in missing dependence structure(i.e., copula \cite{sklar1959}) indicating how multiple attributes co-occur. As a result, the generated synthetic population could contain inner-bias, for example, an impossible combination of two or more attributes can be assigned to one synthetic individual. 

The process of recovering a joint attribute from its marginals alone is inherently challenging, because the structure of the copula is hard to reconstruct \cite{frogner2019}. However, existing works related to creating the synthetic population manage to solve such issues, which fall into two main categories: synthetic reconstruction and combinatorial optimization \cite{chapuis2022}. As for synthetic reconstruction, methods like Hierarchical sampling (HS) \cite{jiang_large-scale_2024}, Iterative Proportional Fitting (IPF) \cite{predhumeau_synthetic_2023} and Iterative Proportional Update (IPU) \cite{ipu2009} recover the joint distributions from an aggregated-level data (e.g., census tract level data) by matching certain attributes' target marginals while preserving the copula structure \cite{choupani2016,lovelace2015}. With respect to the combinatorial optimization \cite{harland2012}, one can extend real individual-level data (e.g., PUMAs Data) or adjust existing synthetic individuals to match target joint distributions via fitness computation procedures and optimized sampling algorithms. 

However, these methods share a commonality, which is that the copula structure is extracted from aggregated-level data (e.g., census tract level data) and real individual-level data (e.g., PUMAs Data), then applied uniformly across regions. The advantage of extracting such structures from existing data is that it works well when regions are demographically similar. In contrast, when the regions are demographically heterogeneous, this fixed copula structure may misrepresent the local dependence and such errors lead to the inner bias in the resulting synthetic population \cite {roxburgh2025}. Another issue caused by the methods mentioned above is that the resulting synthetic population may be spatial non-stationarity \cite{krupskii2018,mondal2024,bastin2023}. But the spatial non-stationarity exists across different regions in the U.S., for example, we quantify this variation for American 2,456 Public Use Microdata Areas (PUMAs), and the results show that PUMA area dependence structure deviates from the national average by measuring a mean total variation distance (TVD) of 0.19, which is far from negligible, and far from uniform across differnt regions(Fig.~\ref{fig:heterogeneity}). This is because in the real world, a university region with distinctive ``young + low-income + high-education'' attributes combination may differ from a manufacturing region with respect to age--income--education combinations.

To handle such copula structures, optimization methods, such as Integer Least Squares Programming, utilized by \cite{lin2023} created the synthetic population for the U.S. using Census Block Group data. In addition, parametric modeling approaches use statistical inference methods to learn the joint relationship among attributes hidden in the data. Such methods generated the synthetic population using predefined copula structures by specifying a set of parameters for the Markov Chain Monte Carlo process \cite{farooq2013} and Bayesian network sampling \cite{zhou_creating_2022}. One main shortage is that the spatial non-stationarities of the synthetic population stay unresolved, because the same set of parameters is used in the population synthesis process.

Recently, generative models have provided the possibility to recover the correct copula structure along with solving the spatial non-stationarities of the synthetic population \cite{ang2025,sane2025}. Among these generative models, denoising diffusion models \cite{ho2020} are particularly well-suited to this task, because with this model, an iterative denoising process can learn the joint distribution without redefined parameter settings like the Markov Chain Monte Carlo process \cite{farooq2013} and Bayesian network sampling \cite{zhou_creating_2022}. Furthermore, the denoising diffusion model is able to support conditional generation to minimize spatial non-stationarities of the synthetic population when generating the population across different regions. As the model can be trained using existing data (e.g., PUMAs data) that comprises different copulas structures and spatial heterogeneities to predict a target distribution and work as constraints, when generating the synthetic population. Therefore, in this paper we would like to create the U.S. synthetic population by utilizing a denoising diffusion model with PUMAs data.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Overview}

The goal of this work is to learn region-specific dependence structures from data, replacing the fixed global template used by standard methods. The Introduction identifies two challenges: (1)~regional copulas vary substantially across space, and (2)~recovering a high-dimensional joint distribution from low-dimensional marginal constraints is fundamentally underdetermined. Our approach addresses both through a three-stage pipeline (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}).

\textbf{Stage~1: Distribution-level representation.}
For each of 2,456 US regions (PUMAs), we construct the empirical joint probability vector $\mathbf{p}\in\Delta^{K-1}$ over $D$ discretized demographic attributes, where $K=\prod_{d=1}^{D}B_d$ is the number of joint cells. Rather than training on individual person records, we treat each region's $\mathbf{p}$ as a single training example, directly aligning the learning objective with the evaluation metric (\S\ref{sec:training_target}).

\textbf{Stage~2: Conditional diffusion learning.}
A denoising diffusion model learns to map demographic constraints---one-dimensional marginals and, optionally, two-dimensional pairwise cross-tabulations---to the full joint distribution. The model is trained under the standard DDPM $\epsilon$-prediction objective, with constraints injected via concatenation at the denoiser input. Four conditioning modes (none, marginal, pairwise, marg+pair) form a controlled ablation ladder that isolates the information contribution of each constraint type (\S\ref{sec:condition_design}).

\textbf{Stage~3: Post-processing and evaluation.}
At inference, 128 independent denoising draws are averaged and passed through softmax to produce a raw probability estimate; one round of post-hoc IPF then enforces exact marginal consistency. The same post-processing is applied to the global-seed IPF baseline, so that any performance difference reflects seed quality alone. Evaluation uses a leave-Michigan-out spatial split to prevent autocorrelation leakage (\S\ref{sec:evaluation}).

Recovering $\mathbf{p}$ from marginals alone is fundamentally underdetermined: $K-1$ unknowns versus $\sum_d(B_d-1)$ constraints. IPF resolves this by computing the I-projection of a seed $\mathbf{q}$ onto the marginal-consistent polytope \cite{csiszar1975}, preserving the seed's interaction structure. When $\mathbf{q}$ is the population-weighted national average, every region inherits the same copula. Our diffusion model replaces this fixed $\mathbf{q}$ with a learned, region-specific estimate.

\subsection{Data and binning}

The data source is ACS PUMS 2023 5-year microdata, covering 2,456 US PUMAs. Each PUMA provides a joint distribution over $D$ demographic attributes, computed from person-level (or household-level) records weighted by survey weights. Two statistical units are used for complementary purposes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{2-var (household):} householder age $\times$ household income, constructed to match the published ACS B19037 table ($4\times16$ bins, $K=64$). This setting provides the Michigan 5-fold cross-validation used for the reported statistical significance test.
  \item \textbf{3/5-var (person):} five person-level attributes---age (AGEP), sex (SEX), personal income (PINCP), educational attainment (SCHL), and employment status (ESR)---discretized at coarse to fine resolutions, yielding $K\in\{32,64,128,256,512\}$. This setting is used to probe scaling behavior across joint dimensionalities.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:var_attr} clarifies the modeling status of each variable. In particular, AGEP and PINCP are not modeled as continuous variables in the diffusion target; they are discretized into bins before joint-table construction.

Joint cell counts use Laplace smoothing ($\alpha=1$), adding one pseudo-count per cell before normalizing to probabilities. At $K=32$ this perturbs cell probabilities by $<0.5\%$. At $K=512$ it prevents $\log(0)=-\infty$ for sparse cells, replacing them with $\log\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{N+K}\bigr)\approx-9$, keeping the log-probability vector well-defined throughout. The two unit systems are not directly compared as absolute quality rankings; they serve different diagnostic roles.

\subsection{Distribution-level training target}
\label{sec:training_target}

The central design decision is the choice of training unit. Rather than training on individual person records---which optimizes per-record likelihoods and provides only weak gradient signal for dependence structure---we treat each PUMA's empirical joint probability vector $\mathbf{p}$ as a single training example. This produces exactly 2,456 training examples, a small-data regime, but directly aligns the learning objective with the evaluation metric: the model minimizes error on the quantity being measured.

\paragraph{Log-probability representation.}
Each probability vector is mapped to log-probability space before training:
\[
  \mathbf{x}_0 = \log(\mathbf{p}+\epsilon), \quad \epsilon=10^{-6}.
\]
This serves two purposes: it compresses the dynamic range of near-zero cells, which would otherwise dominate reconstruction error in absolute probability space, and it produces a distribution over $\mathbf{x}_0$ that is approximately Gaussian---a prerequisite for the DDPM noise schedule to operate correctly.

\paragraph{Z-score normalization.}
The log-probability vector is further standardized per training fold:
\[
  \mathbf{z}_0 = \frac{\mathbf{x}_0 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{train}}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{train}}},
\]
with a small-variance floor of $10^{-6}$ applied to $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{train}}$. This step is critical: without it, the distribution of $\mathbf{x}_0$ is strongly shifted (mean $\approx-11$, std $\approx4$ at $K=512$), placing the training targets far outside the $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ regime assumed by the linear noise schedule. Empirically, omitting z-score normalization collapses TVD to near-random ($\approx0.98$); with it, TVD recovers to competitive levels across all $K$ settings tested.

\paragraph{Denoiser architecture and training.}
The denoiser $\epsilon_\theta$ is a two-hidden-layer MLP with hidden dimensions [1024, 1024], SiLU activations, and a 128-dimensional sinusoidal time embedding. The condition vector $\mathbf{c}$ is concatenated with the noised input $\mathbf{z}_t$ and the time embedding at the first layer. Training minimizes the standard $\epsilon$-prediction MSE loss:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,\mathbf{z}_0,\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\bigl[\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t, \mathbf{c})\|^2\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{z}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, and $\bar\alpha_t$ follows a cosine noise schedule with $T=1000$ timesteps. Optimizer: AdamW ($\text{lr}=10^{-3}$, $\text{wd}=10^{-4}$), gradient clipping at 1.0, batch size 512. Training epochs: 4,000 (2-var) and 30,000 (3/5-var $K\leq256$).

\subsection{Condition design}
\label{sec:condition_design}

Conditions encode the demographic constraints available at test time---statistics that a practitioner can derive from published aggregate data without access to microdata. Conditions are injected by concatenating $\mathbf{c}$ with the denoiser input \cite{ho2022cfg}. Four modes are tested; dimensions are given for the 5-var coarse setting with bin structure $B=(2,2,2,2,2)$, $K=32$:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{none} ($d_c=0$): unconditional generation, testing the model's learned prior over plausible copulas.
  \item \textbf{marginal} ($d_c=\textstyle\sum_d B_d=10$): concatenated one-dimensional marginals, encoding each attribute's frequency independently. Available from any published census marginal table.
  \item \textbf{pairwise} ($d_c=\textstyle\sum_{i<j}B_iB_j=40$): concatenated two-dimensional cross-tabulations for all $\binom{D}{2}$ variable pairs. These directly encode second-order associations---the information that a global-seed IPF lacks.
  \item \textbf{marg+pair} ($d_c=50$): both marginal and pairwise signals concatenated.
\end{itemize}

These four modes form a controlled ablation ladder: each step adds a specific type of constraint, and the TVD reduction between consecutive steps quantifies the information value of that constraint type for copula recovery.

\subsection{Sampling and post-processing}

For each test region, generation proceeds as follows: (1)~draw $\mathbf{z}_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$; (2)~run DDPM reverse denoising with the test region's condition vector $\mathbf{c}$, yielding $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_0$; (3)~apply the inverse z-score transform to obtain $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$; (4)~apply softmax to produce the raw probability estimate $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{\text{raw}}$; (5)~average 128 independent draws to reduce Monte Carlo variance. For all marginal-conditioned modes, one round of post-hoc IPF is then applied, enforcing exact agreement with the test region's one-dimensional target marginals.

Crucially, post-hoc IPF is applied identically to the diffusion output and to the global-seed IPF baseline: both pipelines begin from a seed copula and are adjusted to exactly match target marginals. This design isolates a single factor---seed quality---as the sole source of any performance difference.

The resulting probability vector $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ defines the synthetic population for a target region. Given the region's census-reported population count $N$, each demographic cell $k$ is assigned $n_k=\mathrm{Round}(N\times\hat{p}_k)$ individuals, whose attributes are determined by the cell's combination of discretized variables. This procedure guarantees that the synthetic population reproduces both the target marginal frequencies (via post-hoc IPF) and the learned dependence structure (via the diffusion model's joint prediction).

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\paragraph{Split design.}
The primary evaluation uses a leave-Michigan-out split: Michigan's 68 PUMAs form the test set, and the remaining 2,388 PUMAs serve as the training set. Holding out an entire state prevents spatial autocorrelation leakage: in random splits, neighboring regions sharing labor markets or commuting zones would appear in both training and test sets, inflating apparent generalization. For the 2-var household setting, a Michigan 5-fold cross-validation further partitions the 68 held-out PUMAs into five folds of 13--14 regions, enabling fold-level significance testing.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
The primary metric is TVD $=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_k|\hat{p}_k-p_k|$, where $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ is the generated distribution and $\mathbf{p}$ is the observed PUMA distribution. TVD ranges from 0 (exact recovery) to 1 (no overlap); the statistical independence baseline achieves TVD $\approx0.290$ at $K=32$, providing an upper reference. Cosine similarity is reported as an auxiliary metric.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
(1)~\textbf{Independence}: outer product of target marginals, representing the zero-interaction assumption.
(2)~\textbf{IPF(global seed)}: the population-weighted average joint over all training PUMAs serves as the IPF seed; post-hoc IPF adjusts to target marginals, representing current standard practice.

\paragraph{Statistical test.}
Fold-level comparison uses a one-sided sign test \cite{demsar2006}: under the null that diffusion and IPF are equally likely to win any fold, the probability of diffusion winning all five folds is $(1/2)^5=0.031$.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Generated joint distributions capture region-specific dependence}

The model's output for each target region is a complete joint probability vector $\hat{\mathbf{p}}\in\Delta^{K-1}$, from which a synthetic population is instantiated by assigning person counts to each of $K=32$ demographic cells. Figure~\ref{fig:output_examples} illustrates this product for two contrasting Michigan PUMAs selected at the 90th and 50th percentiles of TVD-to-global (the heterogeneity metric from Fig.~\ref{fig:heterogeneity}): a university-town region whose dependence structure deviates strongly from the national average, and a typical suburban region near the median.

Three levels of comparison reveal how well the generated population matches reality. First, one-dimensional marginals---the age, sex, income, education, and employment composition of each region---are reproduced exactly, because post-hoc IPF enforces this by construction (Fig.~\ref{fig:output_examples}a,d). Second, and more informatively, the two-dimensional cross-tabulations capture region-specific dependence patterns. The university-town PUMA has an unusually high share of college-educated residents (63\%, vs.\ $\sim$40\% nationally), and this elevated education rate reshapes the income--education association: the dominant cells are ``high-education, high-income'' (35\%) and ``low-education, low-income'' (34\%), with ``high-education, low-income'' appearing at a low but non-negligible rate (2.6\%)---a pattern absent from the suburban PUMA, where ``low-education, low-income'' dominates at 54\%. In both cases, the generated cross-tabulation closely matches the observed one (Fig.~\ref{fig:output_examples}b,e). Third, across all 32 joint cells---including tail cells where population shares fall below 1\%---generated and observed person counts track closely, with TVD of 0.021 and 0.015 respectively (Fig.~\ref{fig:output_examples}c,f). These values are consistent with the 68-region test-set average of 0.024 (Table~\ref{tab:cond32}), and the most atypical region is reconstructed as accurately as the typical one.

\subsection{Verification and validation}

Under pairwise conditioning at $K=32$, the model's raw outputs---before any post-hoc IPF adjustment---already recover the correct one-dimensional marginals with TVD $<0.002$ for all five attributes (Fig.~\ref{fig:verification}a). This learned marginal consistency demonstrates that the model has internalized the structural relationship between low-order constraints and the full joint distribution: it produces outputs that are nearly marginal-consistent by construction, rather than relying on post-processing to enforce this property.

To test whether performance generalizes to unseen regions, we conduct a Michigan 5-fold cross-validation. Michigan's 68 PUMAs are split into five folds of 13--14 regions; each fold is held out in turn while the model trains on all non-Michigan regions plus the remaining Michigan folds. Across all five folds, TVD ranges from 0.065 to 0.077, with inter-fold standard deviation of 0.005. This stability indicates that the model captures systematic structure in the marginal-to-joint mapping rather than overfitting to training regions. We chose Michigan (an entire state) as the held-out unit to avoid spatial autocorrelation leakage that would arise from random regional splits.

Training convergence is rapid: TVD improves sharply during the first 3{,}000 epochs, capturing approximately 90\% of the total error reduction. Performance continues to improve slowly through epoch 10{,}000---the overall optimum---after which it stabilizes: the 30{,}000-epoch model falls within 1\% of the 10{,}000-epoch minimum (Fig.~\ref{fig:verification}b). This convergence profile suggests that the marginal-to-joint mapping is efficiently learnable and does not require extensive hyperparameter tuning of the training schedule.

Monte Carlo stability is assessed by varying the number of denoising draws averaged to produce each region's output distribution. Performance improves sharply from 1 to 16 draws as averaging reduces sampling noise, then stabilizes beyond 64 draws. At 128 draws, run-to-run standard deviation across 10 random seeds falls below 0.001 TVD (Fig.~\ref{fig:verification}c), confirming that the reported results are not sensitive to stochastic variation in the generation process.

\subsection{Constraint informativeness drives dependence recovery}

The results above establish that the model produces accurate, generalizable joint distributions, but they do not explain which types of constraints drive dependence recovery. To isolate the contribution of each information source, we perform systematic ablations: the model is trained under four conditions---no constraints, one-dimensional marginals only, two-dimensional pairwise cross-tabulations only, and both combined---and the resulting TVD is measured against the true joint.

For the 5-variable $K=32$ setting, the progression is striking (Table~\ref{tab:cond32}; Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}a): without any constraints ($d_c=0$), the diffusion model learns a prior over plausible copulas, achieving TVD = 0.114---a substantial improvement over statistical independence (0.290) but far from the true dependence. Adding one-dimensional marginals ($d_c=10$) provides local frequency information, reducing TVD to 0.043. Adding two-dimensional pairwise cross-tabulations ($d_c=40$) reduces TVD another 44\% relative to marginal-only (from 0.043 to 0.024), because these constraints directly encode second-order associations that cannot be inferred from univariate information alone.

Why is this progression meaningful? In inverse problems, the fundamental constraint is information: you cannot recover more unknowns than you have constraints. At $K=32$, the joint has 31 free parameters; one-dimensional marginals provide only 10 constraint values ($d_c=10$), leaving 21 degrees of freedom undetermined. Two-variable cross-tabulations add 40 constraint values ($d_c=40$) encoding all pairwise interactions. The model uses this richer constraint set to pin down the higher-order structure.

The model's learned marginal consistency (confirmed in \S2.2) further supports this interpretation: under pairwise conditioning, the raw outputs already satisfy the input constraints, indicating that the model has internalized the exact mapping from constraints to distribution rather than merely fitting surface-level patterns.

Does this ablation pattern hold across different numbers of variables? We test this by computing the pairwise contribution---the TVD reduction from marginal-only to pairwise---across 2-variable to 5-variable settings. \section{Discussion}

The condition ablation reveals a structural insight about synthesis as an inverse problem: the critical resource is not model capacity but constraint informativeness. From none to marginal to pairwise, each additional layer of constraint information reduces error by a large, discrete step. Model architecture matters only at the margin. The advantage reverses at $K=512$, where two mechanisms jointly prevent recovery: cell sparsity ($\sim$14 persons per cell vs.\ $\sim$220 at $K=32$) and constraint insufficiency (128 pairwise parameters against 511 joint unknowns), so that even extending training from 10k to 30k epochs does not close the gap (Supplementary Table~S1, Fig.~S1). This reframes the scaling boundary not as a model failure but as an information-theoretic limit: enriching input constraints is more effective than increasing model capacity or training budget.

The choice of training target---predicting regional distributions rather than individual records---has implications beyond this specific model. Tabular generative models trained on individual samples \cite{kotelnikov2023,stoian2024} optimize per-record quality, which does not guarantee population-level distributional accuracy---a tension between feasibility and diversity that recent work has begun to formalize \cite{kim2022}. For synthesis tasks evaluated on regional joint statistics---the standard in transportation and urban modeling---training directly on the aggregate quantity of interest aligns the learning objective with the evaluation metric, and our results suggest this alignment is decisive.

The dependence heterogeneity finding (mean TVD 0.19 across 2,456 regions) has independent value and provides a quantitative frame for interpreting model performance. The 0.19 TVD represents the average deviation of regional dependence from the global seed that IPF uses. At $K=32$, pairwise-conditioned diffusion reduces TVD from 0.058 (IPF) to 0.024---closing approximately 60\% of this gap. At $K=128$, it closes about 37\%. These numbers give a concrete answer to ``what did the model learn'': it recovered a substantial fraction of the spatial dependence variation that IPF's global seed ignores. This motivates not only learned approaches but also stratified IPF schemes that use cluster-specific seeds.

Limitations are explicit. First, experiments use US PUMS; generalization to other countries depends on comparable marginal systems. Second, pairwise conditions require cross-tabulations that may not always be publicly available, though they can be estimated from microdata samples. Third, the 2-var and 3/5-var settings use different statistical units (household vs.\ person); cross-setting comparisons serve as scaling diagnostics, not same-unit absolute rankings. Fourth, the current model does not enforce marginal constraints architecturally---this is handled by post-hoc IPF, which adds a processing step.

A concrete path forward: enrich constraints before enlarging models. Higher-order cross-tabulations are ideal but often unavailable. Tract-level auxiliary descriptors---built environment, labor structure, university/industrial profiles---offer a feasible route to increase conditional identifiability in high-dimensional settings. More broadly, population synthesis can be viewed as an instance of ecological inference \cite{king1997}---reconstructing individual-level structure from aggregate observations---for which distribution-level generative models provide a natural probabilistic language. Extending the present framework to sub-regional disaggregation, where building-level spatial priors replace marginal constraints as the conditioning signal, is an immediate next step enabled by increasingly available building footprint and land-use data \cite{rineer2025}.

\subsection*{S1. Dimensional scaling and the information-sparsity boundary}

All five configurations with $K\leq256$ show diffusion advantage over IPF. Testing whether this holds at higher dimensionality, we train on $K=512$ (fine-grained 5-variable joint: $4\times2\times4\times4\times4$ bins). Diffusion TVD reaches 0.139 while IPF achieves 0.112, a reversal of $+0.028$ (Supplementary Table~S1).

Two mechanisms drive this reversal. First, \textit{cell sparsity}: at $K=32$, each joint cell contains $\sim$220 persons on average; at $K=512$, only $\sim$14, making empirical probability estimates unreliable as training targets. Second, \textit{constraint insufficiency}: the pairwise condition vector provides 128 parameters, but the $K=512$ joint has 511 free parameters---a coverage ratio of 25\%, versus 129\% at $K=32$. Extending training from 10k to 30k epochs reduces TVD from 0.182 to 0.147 ($-$19\%) but does not reach IPF's 0.112, confirming the bottleneck is information, not optimization. A counterintuitive corollary: pairwise conditioning at $K=512$ (TVD = 0.249) is \textit{worse} than marginal-only (0.147), because the 128-dim pairwise input must reconstruct 511 unknowns without the global-prior support that IPF supplies to the marginal-only pipeline.
